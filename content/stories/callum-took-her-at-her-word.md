Callum, 31, had a theory about the phrase *work has been mad, sorry*. The theory was that it never meant work.

It meant she'd cooled off. It meant someone better had appeared. It meant he had about one message left to be charming enough to reverse it. So he'd do what he always did — send something bigger, funnier, more effortful, a little joke about being ghosted that wasn't really a joke. Or he'd take the hint too hard and disappear, which felt like dignity at the time and was mostly just fear.

Either way, the thread died. He'd chalk it up to her losing interest, which conveniently proved the theory.

## Neve, at the end of a fourteen-hour day

Neve, 29, was an architect three weeks into a planning deadline, and when she typed *barely a person right now*, she meant it in the most literal way available. She liked Callum. She had also answered eleven emails between his last two messages.

He looked at the message for a long time and noticed something uncomfortable: nothing in it actually said she was done. It said she was tired. He'd just never been able to tell the difference, because he'd never once tested the boring explanation first.

So he tried a version he'd never sent before — one that believed her, released her from the obligation to reply well, and still left a door propped open:

> "Noted, and no reply owed. When the brutal month ends I'm buying you the flat white you've earned."

No performance. No audit of how long she'd taken. Nothing for her to manage. It's the kind of line that's obvious once you see it and impossible to find at eleven at night with your thumbs hovering — the sort of thing hintder is genuinely useful for drafting. He still had to be the one to send it.

## What "no reply owed" actually did

She replied in four minutes. Not because the line was clever, but because it was the first message that week that hadn't asked her for anything.

Two nights later she named a Thursday herself. She told him, months on, exactly what had happened in her head: he was the only one who hadn't made her exhaustion into a problem she also had to solve.

Two years later they still guard Thursday. And the lesson Callum keeps handing to friends is unglamorous and free: when she tells you why she's slow, the winning move is almost always to believe her, out loud, and make the next reply easy to send.
