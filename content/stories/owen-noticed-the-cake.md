Delia's inbox was a monument to laziness. Not cruelty — just effort-free sameness. *You're gorgeous. Stunning. Wow, those eyes.* Message after message aimed at her face, as if her face were the only thing she'd decided to put in front of strangers.

She had, in fact, decided a lot. The rooftop photo was her city. The one with the dog wasn't even her dog, but the story was good. And the fourth — the one she almost cut — was her mid-laugh in a flour-dusted apron, a sad, sunken birthday cake slumping on the counter behind her. She'd left it in because it was the most *her* photo she had. Nobody ever mentioned it.

## The one who read past the first line

Owen almost defaulted to the easy line too. She was, objectively, gorgeous — the compliment was sitting right there. But he'd learned the hard way that the obvious line lands in a crowd of identical obvious lines. So he looked at the photos like they were sentences she'd written on purpose, and one of them was practically waving at him.

> "Okay, that cake in your last photo looks like it fought back and won. I need the full story."

It wasn't a slick line. It was just *attention* — proof he'd actually looked. And it worked because it asked her to talk about something she was proud of, instead of something she'd been complimented on a thousand times and had nothing left to say about.

She wrote back three sentences. Then a fourth. Then she was the one asking questions.

## Notice the thing she put in the frame

The turning point wasn't charm. It was that Owen treated her profile as a set of choices rather than a headshot to rate. Everyone else answered the face. He answered the cake — the detail she'd risked leaving in precisely because she hoped someone would.

That's the kind of opener that's easy to miss and easy to find once you're looking: not a compliment on how she looks, but a genuine question about the small, specific thing she went out of her way to show you. It's exactly the sort of line hintder is good at spotting in a profile — the detail everyone else scrolls past. Owen still had to be the one to send it. He just had to notice it first.

A year of Sundays later, something in their kitchen still collapses most weekends. They eat it off the tray with spoons, the way she did in a photo three other guys couldn't be bothered to look at.
