Emmett had one rule, and he'd never once said it out loud: never be the one who wants more.

It worked, in the sense that nothing ever hurt. He kept things breezy. When someone asked what he was looking for, he had a whole repertoire — *seeing what happens, open to whatever, not really thinking about it.* Answers built to give nothing away. Nine years of them.

What he didn't notice was that the vagueness wasn't protecting him from rejection. It was doing the rejecting for him. Women would read *open to whatever* as *not interested in you specifically*, and drift, politely, and he'd file it under bad luck.

## The question, for the hundredth time

Josie asked on a Tuesday, four days into a thread that had been genuinely good. Same question he'd dodged since he was twenty-five: serious, or not?

He typed the usual deflection. Looked at it. Thought about the last three matches who'd faded out somewhere around week two, and how every single one of those conversations had a moment exactly like this in it, where he'd said nothing and she'd stopped asking.

So he deleted it and wrote the true thing instead:

> "Honest answer — the real thing. I'd rather scare you off now than be vague at you for three weeks."

It's not a clever line. That's sort of the point — it's the kind of line that only works because it's true, and the kind hintder is good at helping you find when your instinct is to hedge. It drafts it; you're still the one who hits send. Emmett hit send with his stomach in his throat and then put the phone face-down on the counter for eleven minutes.

## What honesty actually filters

She said the same thing back. Not because the line was persuasive — because she'd been asking that question for a year and getting fog, and someone finally answered it.

They met that Friday at a fish fry on the south side and closed the place down. Two years on, it's still Friday, still the fish fry, still the same order.

Emmett's read on it now is blunt: being vague doesn't keep your options open, it keeps everyone at arm's length, including the one person who'd have said yes. The risk isn't scaring off the wrong people. It's that the right one can't tell you're there.
