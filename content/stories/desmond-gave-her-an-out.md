Desmond was a closer, and he thought that was a compliment.

Every hesitation was a thing to be solved. She's busy this week? He'd offer three other days. She's not sure about dinner? He'd counter with drinks, then coffee, then a walk. He was reasonable, he was flexible, he was relentless — and he could not understand why so many conversations that ended in a confirmed plan also ended in a woman who quietly stopped replying two days before it.

He was collecting yeses. He just never stopped to check whether any of them were real.

## The disaster week

Fiona was thirty-one, an occupational therapist, and in the middle of moving her mother out of a flat she'd lived in for twenty-six years. They'd been messaging for nine days. It was good — dry, unhurried, the kind of thread where neither person is trying to be impressive.

Then she sent the message that would normally trigger the whole machine: *this week is a disaster, I'm terrible company when I'm this tired.* Desmond's thumbs knew exactly what to do. Suggest next week. Suggest something smaller. Keep it alive at all costs.

He looked at it again and noticed something he'd been missing for years: she wasn't making an excuse. She was telling him the truth about her week, which is a strange thing to do with someone you're trying to get rid of.

So instead of negotiating, he gave her the door:

> "Then don't force it. Say the word and we'll park it until things calm down, I'm not going anywhere."

And then — this is the part that mattered — he left the small option on the table without leaning on it. Thursday's open. A short coffee near you, if it would help. No follow-up nudge. No *unless you'd rather...*

## What an exit buys you

She took the coffee. It was supposed to be forty minutes and it turned into the entire waterfront, because a person who hasn't been pushed all week will sometimes talk for an hour about her mother's flat.

Desmond's read on it now is blunt: pressure doesn't get you a yes, it gets you a maybe with a countdown on it. The women who stopped replying two days before the date had never actually chosen to go. They'd just run out of polite ways to decline. Handing Fiona a real out was the only way to find out she genuinely wanted in.

That message is the kind of thing hintder is good at drafting when your instinct is screaming at you to push — it can find the low-pressure version of the line. Desmond still had to be the one who sent it, and then sat on his hands and didn't send another.

Two years on, she cancels things constantly. He tells her it's fine every time. It's the same sentence that got him in the door, and he's never had a reason to stop using it.
