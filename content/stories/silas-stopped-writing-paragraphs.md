Silas was, by every measure he could think of, a good texter. His messages were warm. They were considered. They had structure — an acknowledgement of what she'd said, a thought of his own, a question at the end, sometimes a small joke tucked in the middle.

They were also four lines long, and they kept ending conversations.

He'd watch it happen and not understand it. A promising match, a few exchanges, and then a reply that came slower, and shorter, and then one that didn't come at all. He assumed the problem was him — his personality, his photos, some invisible deficiency the paragraphs were meant to compensate for.

## The problem with a finished message

The problem wasn't that his messages were bad. It was that they were done.

When you answer someone completely — acknowledge every part, offer your own full thought, close it off with a neat question — you've handed them a finished object. There's nothing to pick up. The only thing left to do is match your effort, and matching four thoughtful paragraphs at 11pm on a Tuesday is work. Most people, quite reasonably, don't.

Nadine's profile mentioned sailing, and Silas caught himself drafting the usual: something about how he'd always wanted to learn, a question about where she sailed, a line about Charleston harbour. Complete. Closed. Polite.

He deleted it and sent this instead:

> "Worse how. I need the specific disaster."

Nine words. A hole where the rest of the message should have been. And she climbed straight into it — the capsizing, the four feet of water, the children's sailing class, the child who applauded.

## Nine words and a sandbar

He didn't tidy that up either. He picked the funniest thing in her message and handed it back, and she kept going, and somewhere in there she mentioned a sandbar story she'd clearly been waiting to tell someone.

That's where he stopped texting and named a Thursday.

Finding a short line like that is exactly the sort of thing hintder is good at — you feed it the chat and it hands you a few options in the tone you asked for. But it only drafts. Silas read it, decided it sounded like him, and sent it himself. That part is never automated, and shouldn't be.

A year later their thread is mostly fragments — half-sentences, one-word answers, a photo with no caption. All the long versions happen on the porch, out loud, where they belong.

The lesson isn't *write less*. It's *leave something out*. A message that says everything is a message she can only agree with. A message with a gap in it is an invitation.
